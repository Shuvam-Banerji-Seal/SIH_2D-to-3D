\begin{abstract}
We present an end-to-end system that turns a single drone pass --- one video, no flight planning, no ground control --- into a textured 3D mesh and a dense point cloud and, when a flight log is available, georeferenced metric outputs in the formats problem statement 26158 asks for (OBJ, PLY, LAS, GeoTIFF, glTF, FBX), with a web viewer. Every stage runs on one GPU. A keyframe selector formalises the intuition that consecutive views must be \emph{similar enough to match and different enough to triangulate}: it chains optical flow to measure co-visibility and picks the sharpest frame inside an overlap band $[\tau,\tau+\delta]$. Before any reconstruction it answers whether a pass contains recoverable 3D structure at all, from rotation-compensated parallax measured with \emph{direct} keyframe-pair flow and normalised by an estimated noise floor. The same flow then replaces feature matching: tracks from direct RAFT flow give camera poses within \FlowSfmCentre\,\% of the flight extent and \FlowSfmRot$^\circ$ of SIFT structure from motion, triangulated flow against wide keyframe baselines gives dense depth, a monocular prior calibrated to it fills what flow cannot see up to the range where its extrapolation still holds, and a GPU TSDF fuses the views, keeping surfaces that three depth maps confirm (two on short shots) rather than Open3D's four. A 187\,s video is processed in \FastQutubSeconds\,s against a budget of \FastQutubBudget\,s (15 minutes per 10-minute video), covering \FastQutubCompl{} of the non-sky pixels its keyframes saw. On a straight single-pass track the usual 7-DoF GPS alignment silently rolls the model about the flight axis; levelling on the ground plane and fitting four degrees of freedom removes the failure, and with synthetic GPS on real models the exported mesh is within \GeoNearTrack\,m of the truth near the track. Overlapping what does not depend on each other --- mapping one pass while the GPU tracks the next, writing files while it bakes the next texture --- brings the 55\,s Jal Mahal edit to \SpeedAfter\,s against its \SpeedBudget\,s budget. A resident engine keeps the networks warm, loads a model only when the GPU has room for it, and reconstructs live streams segment by segment; a web console exposes every option and every layer of the result --- mesh, dense cloud, Gaussian splats trained from that cloud, the keyframes and their fused depth --- in one explorer. A slower profile trains depth-supervised 3D Gaussian Splatting. All numbers are produced by scripts in the repository from the run directories.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Operational mapping --- disaster response, inspection, reconnaissance --- often has one chance to fly over a site. Classical photogrammetry assumes the opposite: planned grids, high overlap, several passes and ground control points. Problem statement 26158 asks for an accurate, textured, georeferenced 3D model from \emph{a single pass} of a moving UAV~\cite{sih2026ps26158}.

A single pass is hard for three distinct reasons, and each one maps to a component of our system.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Which frames?} A video carries thousands of highly redundant frames and a few bad ones (motion blur, compression, fades). Sampling at a fixed rate is the common default; it either wastes compute or, where the drone moves fast, leaves gaps that break the reconstruction into pieces (\S\ref{sec:keyframes}).
\item \textbf{Is it 3D at all?} A drone that yaws on the spot, or looks at a distant or planar scene, produces video in which a homography explains every frame pair. No amount of computation recovers depth from such footage; the system should say so before spending it (\S\ref{sec:verdict}).
\item \textbf{Where is it?} A single pass is nearly a straight line. Aligning the model to GPS through camera centres alone leaves the roll about that line undetermined (\S\ref{sec:georef}).
\end{enumerate}
A fourth constraint is time: the statement's budget is 15 minutes for a 10-minute video, and it scores processing speed (20\,\%) next to accuracy (30\,\%) and completeness (20\,\%). Around these we build two GPU pipelines. The \emph{fast profile}, the deliverable, reuses the selector's optical flow (RAFT~\cite{teed2020raft}) for structure from motion (pycolmap's global mapper~\cite{pan2024glomap}) and dense depth, completes it with Depth Anything~V2~\cite{yang2024dav2}, and fuses a textured mesh and point cloud in a GPU TSDF~\cite{curless1996volumetric,zhou2018open3d} (\S\ref{sec:fast}). The \emph{accurate profile} uses spirula-studio's SIFT structure from motion and 3D Gaussian Splatting~\cite{spirulastudio,kerbl2023gaussians} with the Marigold~v2 depth prior~\cite{pavlovic2026marigoldv2} aligned to, and scored against, the SfM points.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i) An overlap-band keyframe selector over chained dense flow with an explicit multiplicity model, and pass segmentation for edited footage.
(ii) A per-pass 3D verdict from direct-flow residual parallax with a data-driven noise floor, validated on a synthetic pure-rotation control; we report where Torr's GRIC~\cite{torr1998gric} fails on flow correspondences.
(iii) A measurement of chained versus direct optical-flow correspondence error against exact ground truth.
(iv) A ground-levelled 4-DoF georeferencing rule with a significance test for sloped terrain, leave-one-out error and a jackknife scale uncertainty.
(v) Structure from motion and dense depth from the selector's optical flow instead of features, with a measurement of the triangulation baselines a single pass offers, and a study of the levers on completeness: the TSDF truncation band, how many views make a surface, and how far a calibrated monocular prior may extrapolate.
(vi) An engineering study of fitting the budget on one GPU: a single decode for analysis and keyframes, a motion-adaptive analysis rate, RAFT without host syncs under CUDA Graphs, GPU texture baking, overlapped mapping and export, and per-stage time and utilisation recorded against the budget.
(vii) A system around it: a warm GPU engine that loads a model only when the card has room for it and outlives CUDA faults and stalled trainers, live reconstruction from a stream in segments, Gaussian splats on the fast profile's poses started from the dense cloud, and a console whose explorer shows every layer of a reconstruction.

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Structure from motion and splatting.}
Incremental SfM as in COLMAP~\cite{schoenberger2016sfm} remains the reference for camera recovery; spirula-studio re-implements an incremental mapper on the GPU and adds sequence-aware matching for video~\cite{spirulastudio}. 3D Gaussian Splatting~\cite{kerbl2023gaussians} represents a scene as anisotropic Gaussians optimised by differentiable rasterisation; gsplat~\cite{ye2025gsplat} provides the rasteriser we use for fly-through rendering.
\paragraph{Keyframe selection.}
Visual SLAM systems insert keyframes by co-visibility and tracking quality, and ORB-SLAM chooses between homography and fundamental-matrix initialisation by model scores~\cite{murartal2015orbslam}; Torr's GRIC formalises that choice~\cite{torr1998gric}. We use dense flow with the forward--backward consistency test of Sundaram et al.~\cite{sundaram2010tracks}.
\paragraph{Monocular depth.}
Marigold~\cite{ke2024marigold} repurposed a latent diffusion model for affine-invariant depth; Marigold~v2~\cite{pavlovic2026marigoldv2} fine-tunes the Qwen-Image-Edit diffusion transformer~\cite{wu2025qwenimage} to predict affine-invariant log depth in one step. Depth Anything~V2~\cite{yang2024dav2} is a strong discriminative alternative.
\paragraph{Compositional reconstruction.}
WorldSculpt~\cite{niu2026worldsculpt} lifts per-object priors into cluttered scenes from posed, masked views; we discuss it as a downstream extension in \S\ref{sec:limits}.

\subsection{GPU ingest}
Frames are decoded by NVDEC through ffmpeg (\texttt{-hwaccel cuda}), resized on the GPU (\texttt{scale\_cuda}) and leave the decoder as raw NV12; conversion to RGB (BT.709, limited range) runs in PyTorch on the GPU. A background thread streams chunks so decoding overlaps optical flow. Keyframes are later re-decoded at full resolution, selected by timestamp in groups of 24 on two concurrent decoder sessions, cropped on the GPU and encoded with nvJPEG.

\subsection{Pass segmentation}
For analysis frames $I_0,\dots,I_{N-1}$ (long side 640\,px, $\approx$12\,fps) we compute RAFT flow $\mathbf{f}_t$ from $t$ to $t{+}1$ and $\mathbf{b}_t$ back, and the share $c_t$ of pixels passing the forward--backward test $\lVert\mathbf{f}+\mathbf{b}\rVert^2 < 0.01(\lVert\mathbf{f}\rVert^2+\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert^2)+0.5$~\cite{sundaram2010tracks}. Inside a continuous camera move $c_t$ stays high; a cut, fade or dissolve collapses it. A \emph{pass} is a maximal run with $c_t \ge 0.35$, no dark or flat frame, and at least 2\,s; its ends are trimmed while they are darker than $0.8\times$ the pass median, removing fade-ins and fade-outs (a fade kills tracks, so without trimming it attracts keyframes, and a fade frame is a meaningless held-out view). Letterbox bars are found before analysis from the per-row and per-column maximum luma over twelve seek-sampled frames and cropped on the GPU: static black would otherwise count as perfectly tracked content, inflating overlap, and as trivially correct pixels, inflating held-out PSNR. Real footage is often edited; a true single pass yields one pass, and SfM rejoins passes that turn out to share views.

\subsection{Overlap-band keyframes}\label{sec:keyframes}
A grid $G$ of points (8\,px spacing) on keyframe $K$ is chained through the consecutive flows; a track dies when it fails the consistency test or leaves the frame. With $A_t\subseteq G$ the tracks alive at frame $t$,
\begin{equation}
\ov(K,t) = \min\Big(\tfrac{|A_t|}{|G|},\; \tfrac{|\mathcal{C}(A_t)|}{|\mathcal{C}|}\Big),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C}$ are the 32\,px cells of frame $t$ and $\mathcal{C}(A_t)$ those containing a live track. The first term falls as $K$'s content leaves the view (forward motion), the second as new content enters it (backward motion), so the measure is symmetric in effect. The next keyframe is the sharpest frame (Laplacian variance relative to a 2\,s rolling median) among those with $\tau\le\ov(K,t)\le\tau+\delta$ before the curve first drops below $\tau$; defaults $\tau=0.75$, $\delta=0.1$. If the overlap drops below $\tau$ within one step, the next frame is taken to keep the chain connected.

\paragraph{Multiplicity.} Under uniform motion a point stays in view for about $1/(1-\tau)$ keyframe spacings, so each surface point is observed by $m\approx 1/(1-\tau)$ keyframes ($m=4$ at $\tau=0.75$) --- the ``two or more frames with a proper threshold of similarity'' that triangulation needs, stated as a design parameter. We report the measured multiplicity, $1+2\bar L$ with $\bar L$ the mean forward track length in keyframes.

\paragraph{Cost.} Only consecutive pairs are flowed, so analysis is linear in video length. An overlap matrix $M_{i,d}=\ov(i,i{+}d)$ for all frames and $d\le d_{\max}$ is built in one sweep: a fresh grid starts at every frame and all live grids advance together in a ring of $d_{\max}$ slots, one batched \texttt{grid\_sample} per frame and a single read-back; selection is then a CPU walk over $M$.

\subsection{Is it 3D? A parallax test}\label{sec:verdict}
For each keyframe $i$ we take its widest partner $j\le i+6$ with $\ov\ge0.3$, compute RAFT flow \emph{directly} between $I_{K_i}$ and $I_{K_j}$ in both directions, and keep consistent grid correspondences $(\mathbf{x}_n,\mathbf{y}_n)$. On a batch of all pairs we fit, by normalised DLT~\cite{hartley1997eightpoint} with Cauchy IRLS, a homography $H$ and a rank-2 fundamental matrix $F$. The residual $\lVert\mathbf{y}-H\mathbf{x}\rVert$ is rotation-compensated parallax: zero for pure rotation or a planar scene, growing with depth variation times baseline. We estimate the per-coordinate noise $\hat\sigma = 1.4826\,\operatorname{median}\sqrt{e_F^2}$ from $F$'s Sampson residuals $e_F$ --- pure noise whenever $F$ is correct, which includes degenerate motion --- and normalise
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SNR} = \frac{\operatorname{median}_n\lVert\mathbf{y}_n-H\mathbf{x}_n\rVert}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{2\ln2}\;\hat\sigma},
\end{equation}
where the denominator is the median transfer residual that isotropic noise in both views leaves, so SNR$=1$ means no measurable parallax. A pass is \emph{3D} when its median SNR over keyframe pairs is at least 2 and GRIC~\cite{torr1998gric} (both models scored on geometric error with $\hat\sigma$) prefers $F$ on at least 60\% of pairs; \emph{degenerate} when the SNR is below 1.5 and its 90th percentile below 2.

\paragraph{Why direct flow.} Chaining consecutive flows makes long-range correspondence cheap but its error accumulates with a systematic per-step bias (\S\ref{sec:exp}, Fig.~\ref{fig:drift}); over a keyframe span the drift reaches the size of the parallax being measured and is correlated across points, which the extra freedom of $F$ partly absorbs. Direct flow keeps the error near 0.1\,px at the same gaps.

\subsection{Structure from motion}
Keyframes are written per pass (\texttt{images/pass\_$k$/}) and reconstructed by spirula-studio's GPU SfM with each pass declared a sequence, so neighbours are matched and trusted first, plus GPU pair selection for loop closure. We use a \emph{single-focal} radial camera per pass: drone cameras have square pixels, and with independent $f_x,f_y$ the weak geometry of forward or orbital motion let the two drift 10--20\% apart for no gain in reprojection error (\S\ref{sec:exp}). Separate passes of an edited video become separate models unless they share views.

\subsection{Monocular depth prior}
Marigold~v2~\cite{pavlovic2026marigoldv2} predicts, per keyframe, $p(\mathbf{u})$ with $\log d(\mathbf{u}) = a\,p(\mathbf{u})+b$ for unknown $a,b$. It runs as upstream: 4-bit NF4 base transformer (what its LoRA was trained on), rank-128 LoRA and fine-tuned VAE decoder, one rectified-flow step, bf16 autocast, at 1024\,px; the quantised base is cached so a load reads $\approx$11\,GB rather than re-quantising 39\,GB. For every registered image the SfM tie points give depths $z_n$ at pixels $\mathbf{u}_n$.

\paragraph{Affine is not enough at landscape scale.} A Cauchy-IRLS fit of $\log z_n = a\,p(\mathbf{u}_n)+b$ works well where the tie points span a narrow depth range, but on drone footage the prior \emph{compresses the far field}: on Jal Mahal it places the far shore at the depth of the palace and the mountain ridge barely beyond it (Fig.~\ref{fig:depth}), so a single slope cannot fit both. We therefore calibrate each image with a monotone map: medians of $\log z$ in quantile bins of $p$, made non-decreasing by weighted pool-adjacent-violators, linear with the affine slope outside the knots. It keeps the prediction's ordering and lets the mapping bend. Both calibrations are scored on \emph{held-out} tie points (5-fold cross-validation per image).

\paragraph{Where the prior saturates.} In a distant pass every tie point may sit where the prediction has saturated at its far end; the correlation between $p$ and $\log z$ is then near zero or negative. The robust fit returns a non-positive slope and the stage writes no depth map for that image, so training is not supervised with wrong geometry.

The calibrated linear depth supervises splat training through spirula's Pearson-correlation depth loss (weight 0.05). Because that loss is affine-invariant in \emph{linear} depth, the calibration --- which only SfM can provide --- shapes the supervision itself, not merely its reporting. \emph{What} is supervised matters as much: outside the range of predictions the tie points cover (sky, featureless far field) the calibration only extrapolates, and a depth invented for the sky asks the trainer to put splats there. The mask therefore keeps pixels whose prediction lies within the 0.5--99.5\% range of the tie-point predictions (plus 5\% margin) and below $3\times$ the 99th-percentile SfM depth. The canonical run below was trained before this mask existed (only the far cut, which removed nothing); the masked maps are evaluated as an ablation (\S\ref{sec:exp}).

\subsection{Gaussian splatting and meshing}
Each SfM model with at least 8 images trains a 3DGS model in spirula-studio (30k steps, half resolution, bilateral-grid exposure correction), with every 8th keyframe held out for evaluation; meshes are extracted from the trained splats with vertex colour and texture atlases.

\subsection{Georeferencing a straight pass}\label{sec:georef}
With GPS positions $\mathbf{g}_n$ (local ENU) for camera centres $\mathbf{c}_n$, the usual fit is the 7-DoF similarity of Umeyama~\cite{umeyama1991}. If the $\mathbf{c}_n$ are nearly collinear the rotation about the line is unobservable and GPS noise sets it; the in-sample RMSE still looks like GPS noise because the unobservable direction does not affect it. We instead estimate the model's ground normal $\mathbf{n}$ by RANSAC on the sparse points (oriented towards the cameras), rotate $\mathbf{n}$ to $+z$, and fit yaw, scale and translation --- scale and yaw by complex least squares on horizontal coordinates, the vertical offset as a mean residual. The full similarity is used only if the track spans two dimensions \emph{and} its tilt against $\mathbf{n}$ exceeds $5^\circ$ by three jackknife standard errors, i.e.\ GPS proves the ground is sloped. We report leave-one-out RMSE rather than in-sample RMSE, and a jackknife relative standard error of the scale as the scale check.

\subsection{Keeping the GPU busy}
Stock torchvision RAFT rebuilds its correlation lookup offsets on the CPU in each of its 12 refinement iterations, divides by a CPU tensor, builds coordinate grids on the CPU, and upsamples the flow after every iteration although inference keeps only the last; at 640\,px a call cost roughly the same whatever the batch size, a sign of launch- and sync-bound execution. Removing these (identical arithmetic, differences $\le$ bf16 noise) and replaying the network from a CUDA Graph raised throughput \RaftSpeedup-fold. The selector's tracking avoids boolean-mask indexing, whose \texttt{nonzero} forces a host sync, in favour of scatter and redirected writes. Every stage runs under an NVML sampler recording utilisation, power, memory and the presence of other processes on the GPU, so no timing is reported without its context.

\subsection{A fast profile for the time budget}\label{sec:fast}
The accurate profile spends hours per clip on splat training and meshing; the problem statement asks for a mesh or point cloud within 1.5$\times$ the video length. The fast profile keeps the selector and replaces everything after it.

\paragraph{One decode.} The analysis decode also delivers every analysis frame at the output size: ffmpeg splits the NVDEC stream, scales both branches with \texttt{scale\_cuda} and stacks them in one NV12 frame, so one pipe carries both and cannot deadlock; the planes are split before colour conversion so chroma does not bleed across the seam, and candidates are nvJPEG-encoded as they stream. The chosen keyframes are then files already written, where a second, seek-heavy 4K pass took 31\,s for a 55\,s clip. Twelve frame pairs one nominal analysis step apart, sampled across the video, give the median flow per step; the analysis stride grows until a step moves about 2\,\% of the width (at least 3 analysis frames per second). The sample videos move 0.5--3\,px per step at 12\,fps.

\paragraph{Structure from flow.} For keyframes $K_1,\dots,K_n$ of a pass at 960\,px we compute direct RAFT flow from each $K_i$ to $K_{i+1},\dots,K_{i+3}$ in both directions. Points seeded on a grid where no track is alive move from $K_i$ to $K_{i+1}$ by sampling the flow at their sub-pixel position (forward--backward consistent only); each step is also predicted by the direct flow from $K_{i-1}$ and $K_{i-2}$, and a track whose chained position disagrees with a prediction by more than 1\,px ends, while agreeing predictions are averaged, so chained drift (\S\ref{sec:verdict}) cannot accumulate. The tracks are written as COLMAP keypoints at full resolution with matches between keyframes up to four apart, verified by RANSAC and mapped by the global mapper~\cite{pan2024glomap}, capped at about twelve tracks per image. The incremental mapper's defaults --- a 16$^\circ$ initialisation angle, 1.5$^\circ$ to triangulate --- reject whole single passes, whose nearby keyframes are 0.1--1$^\circ$ apart at the scene.

\paragraph{Depth from flow, completed by a prior.} With the poses fixed, every consistent flow vector from a reference keyframe to neighbours $d\in\{2,4,8,12\}$ keyframes away is triangulated by the midpoint of the two rays; a depth is kept if the rays meet at $\ge 0.5^\circ$ and it reprojects within 1.5\,px in both views. Consecutive keyframes are too close for this --- their median triangulation angle on Jal Mahal is 0.04--0.4$^\circ$, twelve apart 0.6--1$^\circ$ --- hence the wide gaps. Candidates from all neighbours are fused per pixel (median, then an angle$^2$-weighted mean of those within 5\,\%, at least two views). What flow cannot triangulate (water, far field, low texture) is filled by Depth Anything~V2~\cite{yang2024dav2}, calibrated per image to that image's triangulated pixels with the monotone map of \S\ref{sec:exp}; its sky is exactly zero disparity, and those pixels are never filled.

\paragraph{Fusion, texture and export.} All depth maps are integrated in an Open3D GPU TSDF~\cite{curless1996volumetric,zhou2018open3d} whose voxel is three pixel footprints at the median depth and whose truncation band is twelve voxels (\S\ref{sec:fast-results}). The mesh is textured from the 1920\,px keyframes on the GPU without UV unwrapping: triangles are packed two per square cell of a 4096$^2$ atlas, each triangle takes the keyframe that sees it most frontally and closely without occlusion (a z-buffer of triangle centroids per view), and each texel samples that keyframe at its point on the triangle. Exports are PLY (full density), GLB, textured OBJ/GLB and FBX (a copy with at most 600k triangles), LAS, and a DSM and orthophoto rasterised from the point cloud; with georeferencing, LAS and GeoTIFF are in UTM with the EPSG code (ENU $\to$ ECEF $\to$ UTM, not ENU offsets). A self-contained three.js viewer opens at the middle keyframe's viewpoint and measures distances and height differences.
\paragraph{When the geometry is wrong.} The calibration also tests the geometry it is given. In a view of a real scene the triangulated log-depth spreads with the prior's (at least 0.44 times as much on every healthy model we measured); a collapsed SfM model --- Qutub Minar's largest, where a hovering stretch was merged with a translating one --- put every pixel of 37 of its 39 views within 1--2\,\% of one depth while the prior spanned a factor of three (0.01 times). Filling such a view builds a wall, and on that one Open3D's mesh extraction failed with an illegal memory access in two fresh processes. A view whose ratio is below 0.03, when the prior itself varies (so that a nadir view of flat ground passes), keeps no depth at all; the model is then counted as uncovered rather than meshed as walls. Views that flow could not triangulate --- a short shot's neighbours are too close for the angle test --- take their SfM tie points as the sparse depth instead, through the same test.
\paragraph{Measuring completeness.} Without ground truth, completeness is measured against what the flight saw: the \emph{view completeness} of a model is the share of its keyframes' non-sky pixels whose ray hits the mesh (sky: where the monocular prediction's disparity is below 0.5\,\% of the image's maximum). A run's figure weights each SfM model by its registered keyframes and counts a model that yielded no mesh --- too few views, no depth --- as uncovered rather than leaving it out. Surfaces the pass never faced are invisible to this measure, and the far field beyond three times the triangulated range, which the fill leaves empty rather than extrapolate, counts against it.

\paragraph{Staying inside Open3D.} Open3D's GPU mesh extraction fails once a grid holds more than about 65k active blocks --- \TsdfOkBlocks{} worked, \TsdfBadBlocks{} did not, at any hash-map capacity, and the CPU path segfaults --- with an ``unspecified launch failure'' that poisons the CUDA context for the rest of the process. The voxel therefore grows until the surface estimate stays under 60k blocks, the fused grid is checked and re-fused once if it is still over, and the hash map is held to 100k blocks, the largest size tested safe for several grids in one process; at the quality profile's resolution this changed completeness by under 0.01.

\paragraph{Sampling once.} Before the analysis decode, the stage looked for letterbox bars in twelve frames and probed the motion in twelve frame pairs, one ffmpeg process per sample. Creating a decoder context per process cost more than the decode itself, and each seek decoded from the previous key frame (the clips' GOPs are 5.3\,s): \SampleBefore\,s of Jal Mahal's run. The samples now start on key frames read from the packet index, are decoded on the CPU, and one set serves both tests: \SampleAfter\,s. The viewer's decimated copy is made with a C++ quadric decimator (\DecimFast\,s instead of Open3D's \DecimOpen\,s for a 3.7M-triangle model, as close to the surface).

\paragraph{Time.} Every stage's wall time is recorded next to the budget. Two host-side traps cost more than any kernel: the login shell's \texttt{OMP\_PROC\_BIND=spread} made the first OpenMP runtime pin each process, and every thread and child it later started (the mapper, ffmpeg), to core~0 --- the global mapper got 0.13 cores until re-pinned --- and torch's CPU path spent 48\,s calibrating 97 depth maps with 24 threads per small operation, 3\,s in numpy.

\section{System: a warm engine, live footage and a console}\label{sec:system}
\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/system.pdf}
\caption{The system around the pipeline. The engine keeps the networks on the GPU between runs and executes jobs --- recorded flights or live segments --- one at a time; the console supervises it, samples the GPU and serves every layer of a reconstruction to the explorer.}
\label{fig:system}
\end{figure*}
\paragraph{A warm engine.} \texttt{drone3d engine} is a resident process per GPU (Fig.~\ref{fig:system}). Every stage takes its networks from a process-wide cache: outside the engine nothing changes --- each network is built and freed per use --- while inside it RAFT, Depth Anything and, on request, Marigold stay loaded, one network serves every batch size, and RAFT's CUDA graphs for a resolution are captured once. Runs write the same files as the command line. On Jal Mahal a warm run took \WarmSeconds\,s against 109\,s cold under the same conditions: warmth saves seconds, not minutes; its point is an engine that is always ready and live segments that start at once.

\paragraph{Sharing a GPU safely.} A mapping server is rarely a dedicated machine, so the engine is built to run beside other GPU work. A model is loaded only if NVML shows its measured footprint plus a reserve free at that moment, other processes included; it is never unloaded while a run uses it (the request waits for the run); and before each run the memory-hungry settings --- TSDF budget, flow batch, texture size --- are fitted to the memory then free. Open3D and CUDA faults are sticky: after every job the engine checks that its context still works, and if not it saves its queue and warm list, leaves a marker and exits, and the console starts a fresh engine that re-warms the same models and resumes the queue.

\paragraph{Live footage.} The problem is posed for recorded video, but a model of the first minutes of a flight is useful while the drone is still flying. ffmpeg reads the link (RTSP, RTMP, SRT, UDP, HLS, HTTP, or a V4L2 camera encoded on NVENC) and cuts it at keyframes into fixed-length segments without re-encoding; each closed segment becomes a run on the engine, ahead of other work. With a flight log every segment is georeferenced into one ENU frame, its origin pinned to the log's first fix, so that the segments' models line up in one scene. A recorded flight can be replayed at its own frame rate to rehearse the whole path (\S\ref{sec:live-results}).

\paragraph{The console.} \texttt{drone3d ui} serves a web console. It shows the engine and each model it holds, with load and unload controls; NVML gauges and ten-minute charts, with our processes told apart from other users'; a check of what the machine can do (CUDA, NVDEC and NVENC, nvJPEG, Open3D on the GPU, the global mapper, the splat trainer, the FBX and Blender writers, the weights); and work in flight against the time budget. A run is configured from a video or a stream, a profile, a resolution preset and module switches, and every option of the configuration is available, generated from the configuration's data classes and the comments beside each field so that the form cannot drift from the code. The explorer (also shipped with every export) shows each layer of a reconstruction as a switch: the textured mesh (texture, shading or wireframe), the dense cloud, the Gaussian splats, the flight path with its frusta, the keyframe photos and their fused depth maps as image planes where the drone took them, and the source video, which follows the camera when the explorer replays the drone's own path. Clicking a keyframe puts the camera where the drone was, with its field of view, and lays the photo over the model. Deliverables per model are PLY, OBJ, GLB (y-up, as glTF requires), FBX, STL, a Blender scene with its texture packed, LAS and GeoTIFF, and the web splats.

\paragraph{Keeping the engine alive.} A resident process meets failures a one-video process never does. After a dozen videos in one engine, Open3D's GPU mesh extraction hit an illegal memory access on a grid well inside every limit of \S\ref{sec:fast} --- state accumulated across grids, not the grid itself --- and a sticky CUDA error poisons every later call in the process. Inside the engine the dense stage therefore fuses in a spawned child process, started with the stage so that it boots while depth is computed: a fault kills the child, the model is retried in a fresh one, and the engine's own context and warm networks are untouched; results are identical (Jal Mahal: same completeness and triangle counts) for about 2\,s plus 0.5\,s per model. If the engine's context is nevertheless lost, it saves its queue and warm list and exits, and the console restarts it with the same models warm; we observed exactly this once during the benchmark, recovered in about a second, with the next job at the front of the restored queue. Memory has to stay flat as well: RAFT replays CUDA graphs, one per frame size, and each graph keeps a private memory pool that the allocator cannot return (1--1.5\,GB at 16 pairs of 480\,px). Every video brings its own size, and after the benchmark the engine held 27.5\,GB of reserved memory for 2.1\,GB of live tensors; the three most recently used shapes are now kept, and reserved memory stays flat. External processes need a limit too: a Vulkan splat trainer once waited in \texttt{poll} at its first step for twenty minutes, holding the queue; the runner now kills a trainer whose log has not grown for three minutes and retries it once, and a model that still fails is recorded as such while the run keeps every other model. New code or a new slot count is applied the same way, by draining: the engine takes no new job, lets running ones finish, and hands its queue to its successor. Two further lessons came from checking capabilities for real: ffmpeg lists NVENC whenever it was built with it, but the A100 has no encoder engine, so a one-frame encode decides and live cameras fall back to x264; and a busy engine can take seconds to answer HTTP (its interpreter lock is shared with the run), so the console judges it by its live process rather than by a short timeout.

\paragraph{Testing.} The repository's tests (pytest, \TestCount{} at the time of writing) cover every stage's arithmetic on synthetic data with known answers, the formats' round trips (y-up GLB, STL, LAS with its CRS, GeoTIFF geokeys, the web splat layout and its frame change), the engine's queue, slots, admission, drain and fault paths, the fusion worker's recovery, and the console's API, uploads included. An end-to-end check drives the console in GPU-accelerated headless Chrome: every page, every explorer layer (the canvas must change when it is switched), zoom, orbit, fly, follow-flight, measuring, screenshots, the source video, look-through-keyframe and the depth comparison, and a request for every deliverable; it fails on any console error or failed request. It found three real faults the unit tests had not: uploads failing on an unresolved type annotation, the thumbnail cache listed as keyframes, and textured meshes rendered black after a layer switch.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\textbf{Budget.} On the warm engine \BenchWithin{} of the \BenchN{} sample videos are processed within the budget; the misses are fast FPV flights and clips under half a minute (\S\ref{sec:fast-results}). \textbf{Completeness.} The meshes cover a median \BenchComplMedian{} of what the registered keyframes saw (\BenchComplMin{}--\BenchComplMax{} across the videos); surfaces the single pass never faced cannot be, and the far field rests on a monocular prior. \textbf{No flight logs in the public samples.} The fifteen sample videos are YouTube re-encodes without GPS; georeferencing is evaluated in simulation and with synthetic GPS on real models, and runs on real data only when a DJI SRT, CSV, GPX or JSON log is supplied. Accuracy degrades with distance from the track (7--18\,m at 300\,m and beyond). \textbf{Edited footage.} Each pass of an edit becomes its own model; a shot of three or four keyframes has neighbours too close for the flow triangulation angle and is modelled from its SfM tie points and the prior alone, so its geometry is less certain. \textbf{Water and sky.} Reflections are reconstructed as mirrored geometry below the surface. \textbf{Dynamic objects} are not masked. \textbf{Live segments} cut a camera move at their boundary. \textbf{Licences.} Depth Anything~V2 Large, the fast profile's default prior, is CC-BY-NC-4.0; the Apache-2.0 Small model can replace it. \textbf{Compositional objects.} WorldSculpt~\cite{niu2026worldsculpt} could turn the scene into per-object meshes given instance masks and boxes; we have not integrated it.

\section{Conclusion}
A single drone pass can be turned into a textured, georeferenced mesh and point cloud on one GPU within the problem statement's time budget, provided the system chooses its frames by what they see rather than by the clock, checks that the motion contains parallax before trusting it, reuses the optical flow it already computed for poses and depth instead of matching features, and aligns to GPS with the constraint a straight line cannot provide. The code, configurations and the scripts that regenerate every number in this paper are in the repository.
